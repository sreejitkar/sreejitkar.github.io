The Inverted Stack: Silicon Moats, Habit Loops, and the Economics of Generative AI

September 20, 2026 · 8 min read

Whenever a shiny new technology cycle kicks off, tech people love to recite the same cozy bedtime story: "Hardware is just the dumb pipes; the real money is made in the software layer."

We saw it in the PC boom: Intel built the silicon, but Microsoft took the high-margin spoils. We saw it in the cloud: data centers were expensive concrete boxes, while SaaS (software you rent over the internet) companies printed 80% gross margins—which is simply finance-speak for the percentage of money you get to keep after paying the direct costs of running the service, before paying your office rent or marketing team.

So when generative AI arrived, virtually everyone assumed the exact same movie would play out. The thesis was straightforward: within a couple of years, foundation models would commoditize, cloud providers would drive compute prices into the dirt, and clever consumer apps would capture 80% of the industry's profit pool.

Well, it’s been a few years now, and I have some bad news for that thesis: the movie didn't follow the script.

Recently, I dove deep into the curriculum of Stanford’s MS&E 435 (The Economics of Generative AI), pioneered by Apoorv Agarwal. Apoorv’s structural breakdowns of the AI stack gave me the exact economic framework I needed to connect the dots with what I’ve been wrestling with on the systems engineering side. When you line up the macro financial data alongside the physical constraints of GPU clusters, the reality hits you like a cold glass of water:

The generative AI value chain isn’t flipping into a software paradise. It is stubbornly, unapologetically inverted.

"In cloud computing, chipmakers take 6% of the profits and apps take 70%. In generative AI, chipmakers take nearly 80% of the profits, and apps scramble for the crumbs. Jensen Huang isn't just selling shovels in a gold rush; he practically owns the mountain."

1. The Inverted Pyramid: Why Jensen Huang Smiles in Leather

Let's look at the numbers. Over the past two years, the total generative AI pie ballooned roughly 5x—from ~$90B to about ~$435B in annualized revenue. That sounds like an absolute tidal wave of software innovation. But when you inspect where that cash actually lands, the picture looks bizarre:

  • Semiconductors (~$300B): Overwhelmingly NVIDIA. Their datacenter business annualized to ~$250B, accompanied by Broadcom's ~$34B in custom AI chips and ~$25B in high-bandwidth memory chips. NVIDIA alone accounts for ~80% of this entire tier.
  • Infrastructure (~$75B): The cloud compute vendors. AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, Oracle Cloud, and GPU rental shops like CoreWeave. This is the only tier where providers are actually locked in a knife fight with one another.
  • Applications (~$60B): The actual user-facing apps. And even this tier isn't a bustling democracy: OpenAI and Anthropic alone command ~$45B (roughly 75% of it), leaving the rest to coding powerhouses like Cursor and vertical agent builders like Glean, ElevenLabs, and Perplexity.

In the cloud era, this stack was shaped like a "V": a narrow hardware base at the bottom feeding a massive, highly profitable software crown at the top. In generative AI, it is shaped like an "A": a titanic hardware base gobbling up 70% of total revenue.

And if revenue looks lopsided, look at gross profit:

  • Semiconductor vendors (NVIDIA) run at an eye-watering ~73% gross margin.
  • Cloud infra vendors run at ~55% gross margin (which drops to a measly 25–30% once you factor in the brutal reality that GPUs depreciate and become obsolete in 3 to 4 years).
  • AI apps run at an estimated 33% to 50% gross margin.

Do the math: out of roughly $285B in total gross profits generated across the AI ecosystem, the chip layer pockets ~$225B—almost 79%. Over two full years of breakneck growth, the application layer clawed back barely 4 percentage points of profit share.

Why is this happening? Because of a fundamental systems truth I explored in The Economics of Inference: intelligence does not behave like traditional software; it behaves like physical manufacturing.

In classic SaaS, serving an extra web page or reading a Postgres row costs fractions of a millicent. Once you write the code, sending it to another million users is virtually free. But in generative AI, running inference—which is simply the technical term for executing a trained model to generate answers for live users—carries a punishing physical cost.

When a model generates tokens autoregressively (one word at a time), its arithmetic intensity—the ratio of math operations the GPU gets to perform compared to the number of bytes it has to drag across the memory bus—collapses to ~1.0 FLOP per byte. The GPU's lightning-fast Tensor Cores spend 99% of their time idling, waiting for 140 gigabytes of model weights to crawl through High Bandwidth Memory (HBM).

Add to that the KV cache (Key-Value cache, the model's working memory scratchpad that stores past conversation tokens so it doesn't have to re-read everything from scratch on every single token), and every chat message burns real memory footprint and real kilowatt-hours.

Every time a user asks ChatGPT to write a poem about their cat or rewrite an email to their landlord, physical silicon works hard and generates heat. That is why AI apps don't enjoy 85% software margins: you aren't just shipping frictionless bits; you're operating a high-voltage digital bakery where every croissant costs 15 cents in flour and butter.

2. The Hyperscaler Capex Gamble: $600 Billion of Pure FOMO

Because compute is so brutally expensive, the biggest tech giants are throwing staggering sums of money into capex—short for capital expenditure, which is the mountain of cash companies spend buying physical assets like land, power substations, cooling towers, and server racks before making a dime.

The top hyperscalers spent ~$443 billion in capex in 2025. In 2026, that number is projected to scream past $600 billion, with ~75% ($450B!) aimed directly at AI compute.

If you listen to the earnings calls, the Big Tech CEOs sound remarkably like poker players who pushed all their chips into the middle of the table:

  • Andy Jassy (Amazon): "As fast as we're adding capacity right now, we're monetizing it."
  • Sundar Pichai (Alphabet): Claimed AI is driving growth across the board, but also admitted there are "elements of irrationality" in the current spending boom (which is polite corporate speak for: yes, this is getting a little crazy).
  • Mark Zuckerberg (Meta): Classic Zuck pragmatism—he noted that aggressively front-loading capacity is the only logical move, because if they over-build, they can just slow down purchases later and let their apps grow into the hardware.

In other words, the fear of missing the boat is 100x more terrifying to these companies than the fear of wasting $50 billion on extra silicon.

To avoid being permanently held hostage by NVIDIA's 75% profit margins, every hyperscaler is racing to build their own ASICs—an acronym for Application-Specific Integrated Circuits, which are custom microchips designed to do one specific job (like multiplying matrices for AI) rather than being general-purpose graphics cards:

  • Google TPU (Ironwood v7): The granddaddy of custom AI silicon. Anthropic ordered up to 1 million TPU chips worth tens of billions, and Google is now selling TPUs as standalone cloud hardware. Reportedly, competitive heat from TPUs has forced NVIDIA to discount merchant GPUs by up to 30% for certain key accounts.
  • Amazon Trainium: AWS has deployed 1.4 million Trainium2 chips, and its custom chip business already crossed a $10B run-rate. Trainium3 (built on 3nm nodes) is already committed through mid-2026.
  • Microsoft Maia, Meta MTIA, and OpenAI's Broadcom deal: Microsoft launched Maia 200 to power slices of ChatGPT; Meta acquired Rivos for RISC-V designs; and OpenAI signed a monster 10-gigawatt custom ASIC deal with Broadcom alongside billions with AMD.

Naturally, Jensen Huang publicly shrugged off custom ASICs as "noncompetitive," noting that most custom chip projects historically get canceled. And honestly, can you blame him? If I were selling shovels at 75% gross margins, I’d tell everyone digging their own shovels was a terrible idea too!

My conjecture? Custom ASICs will win substantial territory in inference, where predictable workloads make specialization easy. But for training frontier frontier models, NVIDIA’s CUDA ecosystem and NVLink interconnects remain an ironclad moat. Shovels will stay lucrative for a long time.

3. Consumer AI is a Two-Horse Race (And Download Charts Lie)

Now, let's step away from the server farms and look at everyday consumers.

Two years ago, the common belief was that incumbents would effortlessly crush startups because distribution always beats product quality. The thinking was: Google has 4 billion Android and Search users; Meta has 3 billion WhatsApp and Instagram users. A standalone chatbot would never survive.

That prediction turned out to be dead wrong.

Total consumer AI usage exploded from ~100 million WAU (Weekly Active Users, the standard metric for how many distinct humans open an app in a 7-day window) in early 2024 to over 1.2 billion WAU in 2026. That is a 20x spike in two years. No category in consumer internet history has ever scaled this fast.

Yet despite hundreds of AI apps launching, the consumer market collapsed into a ruthless power law:

  • ChatGPT: ~900 million WAU. It commands roughly 70% of the entire consumer AI market. And it built that installed base completely from scratch—no pre-installed operating system, no default browser bundle, no existing social graph.
  • Google Gemini: ~200 to 250 million WAU (~15–20% share). A solid second place, but remember: Gemini is riding the back of Android defaults and Google's 4-billion-user ecosystem.
  • The Long Tail: Everyone else—DeepSeek, Grok, Claude, Perplexity, Character AI—is left fighting over the remaining ~10%.

A concept from Apoorv Agarwal’s course notes that really stuck with me is the "Four Seasons of AI Downloads". If you only look at App Store download charts, you'd think OpenAI is constantly about to get disrupted:

  • Winter 2025 (China): DeepSeek drops an incredible open-weights model. Weekly downloads skyrocket past 25 million. Tech Twitter panics: "Is OpenAI finished?!" Within weeks, downloads plummet back to Earth.
  • Spring 2025 (xAI): Grok gets heavy promotion on X (Twitter), spikes up the App Store rankings, grabs headlines, and then cools off.
  • Summer 2025 (Google): Gemini launches its viral image generation feature (the "nano banana" model) around Google I/O. Downloads spike above 20 million on viral meme-sharing, then quickly retreat.
  • Every Single Season (OpenAI): Through all the noise, ChatGPT's adoption line just keeps quietly marching up and to the right.

Here is my takeaway: downloads are like first dates; weekly active usage is someone actually showing up to dinner every Sunday night.

People will impulsively download any novelty app that turns their dog into an anime character or writes a spicy roast. But attention is cheap to rent. Building a daily, unbreakable habit is astronomically hard.

4. The Rare "Smile Curve" and Cognitive Utilities

This brings us to engagement: are people actually using these tools, or just clicking around out of curiosity?

To evaluate this, analysts look at DAU:MAU—the ratio of Daily Active Users to Monthly Active Users. Think of it as a product's "stickiness meter": if your DAU:MAU is 50%, the average monthly user is checking your app 15 days out of the month.

\text{ChatGPT DAU:MAU} \approx 45\% \quad \text{vs.} \quad \text{Gemini DAU:MAU} \approx 22\%

That is more than a 2x gap in daily depth. Gemini reports big monthly numbers, but when it comes to people integrating the tool into their daily lives, ChatGPT is in a different league.

Even crazier is ChatGPT's WAU:MAU (weekly stickiness). In mid-2023, ChatGPT was hovering at ~50%. In traditional consumer tech, your engagement ceiling is supposed to be fixed by your product category: social media is high, productivity tools are medium, utility tax apps are low.

ChatGPT completely smashed that theory. Its weekly engagement climbed from ~50% in 2023 to 82% today—a 30-point leap at massive scale. That puts it right beside everyday essentials like Gmail (78%), Spotify (79%), and Instagram (92%).

Then there's the retention curve. When you plot user retention over time, almost every app on Earth shows one of two shapes: it decays to zero, or it flattens out into a horizontal floor.

Almost nothing exhibits a true Smile Curve—where retention drops initially, and then curves back upward over subsequent months because older users reactivate.

In modern software history, the only products that reliably pull off a smile curve are core internet utilities like Chrome and Gmail. And now, ChatGPT.

At Week 4, ChatGPT holds onto ~66% of its users (two out of every three people who download it are still using it a month later). Compare that to Character AI at 48%, Gemini at 44%, Claude at 41%, and Perplexity at 24%.

Why does ChatGPT smile? Because it doesn’t rely on a dopamine-fueled social feed or pushy notifications pleading with you to open the app. You don't scroll ChatGPT because you're bored in an elevator. You open it with cold, deliberate intent: to write code, draft a contract, parse a messy spreadsheet, or untangle a tricky concept.

And every time OpenAI drops an architectural upgrade—whether it's advanced voice, canvas editing, deep reasoning, or persistent memory—it solves another real-world headache and pulls lapsed users right back into the habit loop. It has become a cognitive utility: a thinking companion you can't imagine working without.

5. The Attention Frontier: Why Ads Will Outscale Subscriptions

So we have 900 million weekly active users forming daily habits. That brings us to the trillion-dollar question: how do you actually make money from this?

Right now, ~95% of ChatGPT's users sit on the free tier. Selling $20/month Plus subscriptions has built an impressive multi-billion-dollar business, but at consumer internet scale, subscriptions hit a wall. Most humans simply refuse to pay recurring monthly software subscriptions.

Look at the titans of the web:

  • Google’s ad business (~$295B+) is roughly 5x Netflix’s total revenue.
  • Meta’s ad machine is roughly 4x Netflix.
  • Even Amazon’s ad business, which barely existed a decade ago, generates more revenue than all of Netflix subscriptions.

The raw material of monetization is time. Over the last two years, total time spent inside generative AI apps grew 10x, and ChatGPT commands 68% of that total attention, with active mobile users spending ~16 minutes per day.

The advertising formula is straightforward:

\text{Ad Revenue} = \text{Total Time} \times \text{Ad Load} \times \text{Price of Ads (CPM)}

Here, Ad Load is how many ads you inject into a session, and CPM (Cost Per Mille, the Latin word for thousand) is the dollar price advertisers pay for every 1,000 views.

And this is where AI assistants have an unfair superpower:

  • Google sells Intent: You type "best term life insurance" into a search bar; Google matches that single search query with high-bidding advertisers ($15 to $200+ CPMs, yielding ~$84 annual revenue per user).
  • Meta sells Boredom: You scroll an endless feed; Meta uses your social behavior and interest graph to guess what you might like (~$57 annual revenue per user).
  • AI Assistants sell Structured Context: When someone chats with an AI assistant, they don't just type keywords; they provide rich constraints, budgets, and personal nuances: "I need a quiet hotel in Kyoto near public transit for a family of four with a toddler, under $300 a night."

That intent signal is cleaner and more actionable than almost anything on the web. That’s why early pilot placements in conversational interfaces have cleared $60+ CPMs.

Let's make a reasonable conjecture: if ChatGPT can monetize its ~850M free users at just $30 in ad revenue per user per year—which is less than half of Google's $84 and well below Meta's $57—that unlocks an immediate $25 billion annual advertising business.

This also exposes a fascinating corporate chess match between OpenAI and Google:

Google DeepMind CEO Demis Hassabis went on record at Davos saying he was "surprised" that OpenAI was rushing forward with ads in ChatGPT, and Google executives have insisted Gemini will remain ad-free.

Well, of course Google can say that! Google has a $295B search advertising money printer in the basement. They can comfortably treat Gemini as a free loss-leader to keep users inside the Google ecosystem while funding the astronomical electricity and GPU bills with Search profits.

OpenAI doesn't have a $295B search engine paying the rent. With no legacy cash cow, they must monetize the interface directly.

The real challenge for OpenAI won't be finding advertisers; it will be preserving user trust. When people treat an AI assistant like an intellectual sounding board or personal confidant, the last thing they want is the bot suddenly pitching mattress discounts in the middle of a delicate conversation. The winners will be the ones who make commercial recommendations feel like native, context-aware assistance rather than annoying banner interruptions.

Closing Thoughts: The Decade Ahead

Working through Apoorv Agarwal’s frameworks in Stanford MS&E 435 while hacking on low-level inference systems led me to three guiding conclusions about where we stand:

  1. Hardware gravity is real, and it’s not letting go anytime soon. The physical laws of memory bandwidth, thermal dissipation, and silicon packaging mean chipmakers will continue claiming the lion's share of profits for years. Anyone forecasting an immediate software margin explosion is ignoring the physics of token generation.
  2. Viral download spikes are vanity; weekly retention is sanity. The AI graveyard will be filled with apps that hit #1 on the App Store for 72 hours before users uninstalled them. The only defensible moat in consumer AI is becoming an embedded cognitive habit.
  3. The ultimate winners will marry systems engineering with attention economics. The enduring platform giants won't just be prompt engineers wrapping someone else's API. They will be full-stack organizations that co-design custom silicon and memory architectures to slash inference COGS, while transforming high-intent conversational attention into natural, trusted monetization.

The AI stack will eventually flip. Software always eats the world in the long run. But don't expect a quick, painless transition. It’s going to be an arduous, decadal migration—fought token by token, cycle by cycle, and habit by habit.
